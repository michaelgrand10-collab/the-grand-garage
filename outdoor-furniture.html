The short buying guide: what resists rust and humidity

Rust is iron oxide, so it only happens to iron and steel. The goal near the coast is simple: keep iron out of the frame and the hardware, or keep it sealed perfectly. Here's how each material behaves.

Powder-coated aluminum

Aluminum isn't iron-based, so it doesn't rust. It's light and handles rain, sun and humidity with little more than soap and water. Salt air can still dull or pit bare aluminum, so the powder coat matters: look for even coverage and smooth, coated welds, and touch up chips when you see them. Consumer Reports also advises stainless or aluminum fasteners. Light pieces can move in a storm, so stack or stow them before high wind.

HDPE poly lumber (POLYWOOD-style)

Solid high-density polyethylene with no wood fibers in it. It doesn't absorb moisture, and POLYWOOD's residential warranty covers its lumber against splintering, cracking, chipping, peeling and rot for 20 years. It's heavy enough to stay put in a breeze. Care is soap, water and a soft brush; POLYWOOD says a pressure washer up to 1,500 psi is fine, but don't sand it. Wirecutter's long-term HDPE set also uses marine-grade stainless hardware.

Resin wicker over aluminum

"All-weather wicker" is plastic strands woven over a metal frame. Wirecutter notes that the frame can be steel or aluminum, and recommends HDPE strands over cheaper PVC, which unravels. In Florida, check the frame: resin over an aluminum frame won't rust, while resin over steel can rust from the inside where you can't see it. If a listing just says "metal frame," assume steel.

Teak (and acacia) with care notes

Teak's natural oils let it sit outside untreated; it weathers to a silver-gray patina that This Old House says doesn't affect its strength. To keep the honey color, clean it and use a teak oil or sealer with UV protection, and skip varnish, which blisters and flakes. Acacia looks similar but needs regular re-oiling (HGTV) and does best under a roof or a breathable cover. Check what the hardware is made of, too.

304 vs. 316 stainless hardware

The screws and bolts are where "rust-proof" furniture often fails first. 304 stainless (also sold as 18/8) is fine inland. Within reach of salt spray, 316 is the safer spec because its added molybdenum resists the chloride pitting and tea-colored staining salt causes (per IMOA, the molybdenum industry association). Rinse hardware with fresh water now and then, and swap any rusting screw for a 316 one.

Why plain steel and iron rust near salt air

Steel and wrought iron are mostly iron. Their paint or powder coat is the only thing between them and the air, and salt speeds up corrosion wherever that coating chips, scratches or was missed at a weld. Consumer Reports suggests checking iron pieces for missed paint spots and fully welded or capped joints. Inland it can be managed; near the coast it means constant upkeep, which is why we skipped steel-frame sets here.

Florida checklist: frame of aluminum, HDPE or teak; hardware that's stainless (316 if you're near the water); cushions with quick-dry foam and covers you can unzip; and a breathable cover or a dry spot for hurricane season. Before a named storm, bring light pieces inside or tie them down.
